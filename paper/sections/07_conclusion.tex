\section{Conclusion}

A broader technical standard can improve interoperability and diffuse common technological improvements across products. It can also change what firms choose to innovate. This paper isolates that portfolio response by allowing competing firms to allocate a fixed R\&D capacity between common-layer and proprietary innovation after the regulator chooses the common scope.

In the quadratic baseline, firms strictly reduce common-layer R\&D as scope expands because their improvements increasingly benefit a rival. More generally, with a differentiable increasing strictly concave innovation technology, private common-layer effort is globally nonincreasing in scope, while a coordinated symmetric allocation is globally nondecreasing. When two compared policy values both induce interior optima, the corresponding order comparisons are strict; no pointwise derivative sign is claimed for an arbitrary general technology. This appropriability wedge feeds back into the regulator's policy problem. Complete standardization is optimal for every fixed positive symmetric common-R\&D allocation and when the regulator coordinates the symmetric R\&D composition while Bertrand pricing remains decentralized. With decentralized endogenous composition, sufficiently strong product-market rivalry yields a unique interior scope in the frozen quadratic baseline.

The policy interpretation is instrument constrained. Selective standardization is not desirable because interoperability is intrinsically costly in the model. It arises because scope is the regulator's policy instrument and broader transferability weakens private incentives to invest in the common layer. The paper does not solve an unconstrained first best and does not establish that selective scope would dominate complete standardization combined with an innovation subsidy, licensing rule, or other appropriation instrument.

The broader implication is that standardization scope and innovation portfolios should be analyzed jointly. A regulator that evaluates standardization while holding the innovation portfolio fixed may favor complete scope even in an environment where firms' equilibrium portfolio response makes a narrower common scope optimal. Future empirical work could examine R\&D, patent, or product-development portfolios around changes in the scope of common technical interfaces, with particular attention to downstream substitutability and to whether common-layer innovative activity becomes weakly less prominent after scope expands.
