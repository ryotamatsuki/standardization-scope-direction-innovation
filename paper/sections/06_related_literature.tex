\section{Related Literature and Contribution Boundary}

The paper sits at the intersection of four literatures: compatibility and endogenous product design, standards and innovation incentives, the direction of innovation, and the allocation of innovative effort across technological activities.

First, compatibility need not be welfare improving once product design is endogenous. \citet{Boom2001} studies a compatibility--product-selection--pricing game and shows that endogenous product selection can overturn compatibility gains. \citet{Ruiz2004} studies international standardization policy with differentiated products and endogenous product characteristics. These papers establish that firms can redesign products in response to compatibility policy. Our adjustment margin is different: product substitutability is fixed, while firms reallocate a scarce R\&D budget between a common technological layer and a proprietary layer.

Second, a substantial literature links compatibility and standard setting to innovation incentives. \citet{BondSmith2019} studies compatibility, endogenous R\&D, and commercialization in markets with network effects. \citet{MaruyamaZennyo2017} analyzes process innovation and endogenous application compatibility. \citet{HeywoodWangYe2022} compares R\&D rivalry and cooperation when compatibility is endogenous. \citet{Llanes2024} studies incentives to develop technologies for inclusion in a technical standard and shows that standardization can generate insufficient or excessive innovation. In a dynamic general-equilibrium setting, \citet{AcemogluGanciaZilibotti2012} analyze competing engines of innovation and standardization and characterize constrained optimal standardization. Their mechanism operates through dynamic growth and technology adoption. The present contribution is therefore not the generic second-best claim that standardization may optimally be restricted; it is the static scope-to-portfolio feedback generated by transferability and downstream competition.

Third, the direction of innovation is itself a developed theoretical object. \citet{BryanLemus2017} study a fixed stock of researchers allocated across research projects and show how racing and underappropriation distort research direction. Their trade-expansion application changes market size, entry, and research competition and thereby changes the direction of research. That channel is close enough that endogenous allocation and underappropriation cannot be counted as novel here. The present model instead fixes two downstream firms and lets a regulator choose standard scope, which directly changes the transferability of common-layer improvements and thereby the private common-versus-proprietary portfolio before the regulator's own scope choice is evaluated.

Fourth, allocation of innovative effort across alternative activities is also established. \citet{KretschmerReitzig2013} model firms' R\&D diversification in emerging standard settings, where firms trade off cooperation on a joint standard against own activities. \citet{daSilva2024} studies scarce managerial resources allocated across internal and external R\&D and shows how spillovers and substitutability across research approaches affect the allocation. Evidence on modular product innovation likewise suggests that external interface standards can redirect innovative attention toward internal interfaces and modules \citep{ChenLiu2005}.

The contribution of the present paper is therefore deliberately result-level rather than thematic. A regulator chooses a continuous standardization scope before firms allocate a fixed innovation capacity between common and proprietary layers. Greater transferability lowers the private relative return to common innovation, while the coordinated symmetric allocation moves weakly in the opposite direction. This endogenous portfolio feedback changes the policy ranking. For any fixed positive symmetric common-R\&D allocation, complete standardization is optimal; complete scope is also optimal when the regulator coordinates the symmetric R\&D composition while Bertrand pricing remains decentralized. Yet under decentralized endogenous composition, the frozen quadratic baseline delivers a unique rivalry threshold above which the optimal scope is interior. The contribution is this scope-to-portfolio-to-policy-reversal architecture and its exact threshold characterization, not the broader claims that compatibility affects innovation, that innovation direction is endogenous, that underappropriation changes research direction, or that partial standardization can be optimal.
