\section{Equilibrium and the Direction of Innovation}

We solve the game backward. Under condition~\eqref{eq:R}, every feasible upstream history has a unique active-product pure-strategy price equilibrium. The first-order conditions are
\begin{equation}
 2p_i-\rho p_j=A_i-\rho A_j,
\end{equation}
which yield
\begin{equation}
 p_i^*=\frac{(2-\rho^2)A_i-\rho A_j}{4-\rho^2}.
 \label{eq:price}
\end{equation}
Demand satisfies $q_i^*=p_i^*/(1-\rho^2)$, so equilibrium profit is
\begin{equation}
 \pi_i^*=\frac{\left[(2-\rho^2)A_i-\rho A_j\right]^2}
 {(4-\rho^2)^2(1-\rho^2)}.
 \label{eq:profit}
\end{equation}
Condition~\eqref{eq:R} makes the bracketed term positive for all feasible histories. The Appendix also rules out separate pure-strategy boundary price equilibria and nonnegative deviations that would foreclose the rival. Hence maximizing profit with respect to $x_i$ is equivalent to maximizing the linear quality index $(2-\rho^2)A_i-\rho A_j$.

Using \eqref{eq:quality} and dividing by $2-\rho^2>0$, firm $i$ solves
\begin{equation}
 \max_{x_i\in[0,E]}
 \left(1-\nu b\right)g(x_i)+g(E-x_i),
 \label{eq:rdproblem}
\end{equation}
where $\nu$ is defined in \eqref{eq:nu}. The rival's R\&D choice drops out of firm $i$'s best response. Economically, one unit of common-layer R\&D improves the firm's own product but, through the standard, also improves its rival. The term $\nu b$ is the private competitive penalty attached to that transfer.

In the quadratic baseline the objective in \eqref{eq:rdproblem} is strictly concave. Under \eqref{eq:nuy}, the unique optimum is interior for every $b\in[0,1]$ and equals
\begin{equation}
 x^F(b)=\frac{y-\nu b}{\kappa(2-\nu b)}.
 \label{eq:xprivate}
\end{equation}
Because each firm's best response is independent of the rival's allocation, the R\&D subgame has a unique symmetric equilibrium in the frozen parameter region.

\begin{proposition}[Private R\&D reallocation]\label{prop:private}
Suppose \eqref{eq:yregion}, \eqref{eq:nuy}, and \eqref{eq:R} hold. In the quadratic baseline,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{dx^F}{db}
 =-\frac{\nu(2-y)}{\kappa(2-\nu b)^2}<0,
 \qquad
 \frac{dz^F}{db}>0.
\end{equation}
Thus broader scope redirects a fixed private R\&D budget from common-layer toward proprietary innovation.
\end{proposition}

The directional mechanism extends beyond the quadratic technology, but the correct general statement is an order comparison rather than a pointwise derivative theorem. Let $g$ be differentiable, increasing, and strictly concave on $[0,E]$. Define
\begin{equation}
 f_F(x,b)=(1-\nu b)g(x)+g(E-x)
 \label{eq:generalprivateobjective}
\end{equation}
and the coordinated symmetric objective
\begin{equation}
 f_S(x,b)=(1+b)g(x)+g(E-x).
 \label{eq:coordinatedproblem}
\end{equation}
Strict concavity gives a unique maximizer of each problem for every $b$.

\begin{proposition}[Directional private--coordinated wedge]\label{prop:wedge}
Let $g$ be differentiable, increasing, and strictly concave on $[0,E]$. For any $b_2>b_1$,
\begin{equation}
 x^F(b_2)\leq x^F(b_1),
 \qquad
 x^S(b_2)\geq x^S(b_1).
 \label{eq:generalweakordering}
\end{equation}
If the private optima at both $b_1$ and $b_2$ are interior, then
\begin{equation}
 x^F(b_2)<x^F(b_1).
 \label{eq:generalstrictprivate}
\end{equation}
If the coordinated symmetric optima at both $b_1$ and $b_2$ are interior, then
\begin{equation}
 x^S(b_2)>x^S(b_1).
 \label{eq:generalstrictcoordinated}
\end{equation}
No pointwise sign for $dx^F/db$ or $dx^S/db$ is asserted for arbitrary general $g$.
\end{proposition}

To see the global ordering, for $b_2>b_1$,
\begin{equation}
 f_F(x,b_2)-f_F(x,b_1)=-\nu(b_2-b_1)g(x),
 \label{eq:privatedifferences}
\end{equation}
which is decreasing in $x$, whereas
\begin{equation}
 f_S(x,b_2)-f_S(x,b_1)=(b_2-b_1)g(x),
 \label{eq:coordinateddifferences}
\end{equation}
which is increasing in $x$. Decreasing and increasing differences therefore give the weak order statements, including at corners. The Appendix gives a direct proof of strict ordering when both compared optima are interior without invoking $g''$.

At an interior optimum the corresponding first-order conditions are
\begin{equation}
 (1-\nu b)g'(x^F)=g'(E-x^F),
 \label{eq:privategeneral}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
 (1+b)g'(x^S)=g'(E-x^S).
 \label{eq:socialgeneral}
\end{equation}
These first-order conditions are used for the strict order comparison across two policy values, not to claim differentiability of the optimizer with respect to $b$. Under the quadratic technology, the coordinated symmetric solution is
\begin{equation}
 x^S(b)=\frac{y+b}{\kappa(2+b)}.
 \label{eq:xsocial}
\end{equation}

\subsection{The regulator's scope choice}

We first hold the R\&D composition fixed symmetrically at $x_1=x_2=\bar x$. Symmetric quality is
\begin{equation}
 A^{FIX}(b)=a+\theta\left[(1+b)g(\bar x)+g(E-\bar x)\right].
\end{equation}
If $\bar x>0$, then $\partial A^{FIX}/\partial b=\theta g(\bar x)>0$.

\begin{proposition}[Fixed symmetric-allocation benchmark]\label{prop:benchmark}
For every fixed symmetric common-R\&D allocation $x_1=x_2=\bar x>0$, welfare is strictly increasing in scope and the regulator chooses
\begin{equation}
 b^{FIX}=1.
\end{equation}
If $\bar x=0$, the regulator is indifferent over $b$.
\end{proposition}

Now substitute the decentralized private R\&D equilibrium \eqref{eq:xprivate}. Since symmetric welfare is strictly increasing in symmetric quality, the policy problem is equivalent to maximizing
\begin{equation}
 F(b)=\kappa\left[(1+b)g(x^F(b))+g(E-x^F(b))\right].
 \label{eq:F}
\end{equation}
Direct calculation gives
\begin{equation}
 F''(b)=
 -\frac{\nu(2-y)^2(2b\nu^2+b\nu+2\nu+4)}{(2-b\nu)^4}<0,
 \label{eq:Fpp}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
 F'(0)=\frac{y(4-y)}{8}>0.
 \label{eq:Fp0}
\end{equation}
Thus zero standardization is never optimal in the frozen baseline region. At $b=1$, define
\begin{align}
 H(y,\nu)={}&\nu^3+2\nu^2y^2-8\nu^2y+2\nu^2 \\
 &+\nu y^2-4\nu y+16\nu+2y^2-8y.
 \label{eq:H}
\end{align}
Then
\begin{equation}
 F'(1)=-\frac{H(y,\nu)}{2(2-\nu)^3}.
 \label{eq:Fp1}
\end{equation}
Moreover,
\begin{equation}
 H(y,0)=2y(y-4)<0,
 \qquad
 H(y,y)=2y(y-2)^2(y+1)>0,
\end{equation}
and $H_\nu>0$ on $0<\nu<y<1$. Hence for every $y\in(1/2,1)$ there is a unique threshold $\bar\nu(y)\in(0,y)$ satisfying $H(y,\bar\nu(y))=0$.

\begin{proposition}[Selective standardization]\label{prop:selective}
Suppose \eqref{eq:yregion}, \eqref{eq:nuy}, and \eqref{eq:R} hold. For each $y\in(1/2,1)$ there exists a unique $\bar\nu(y)\in(0,y)$ such that
\begin{equation}
 \nu\leq\bar\nu(y) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad b^*=1,
\end{equation}
while
\begin{equation}
 \bar\nu(y)<\nu<y \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 0<b^*<1.
\end{equation}
In the latter region the interior policy is unique. This global uniqueness and threshold characterization are claims about the quadratic baseline only.
\end{proposition}

Because $\nu=\rho/(2-\rho^2)$ is increasing in $\rho$, stronger downstream substitutability moves the economy toward the selective-standardization region. Rivalry increases the private cost of transferring the benefit of common R\&D to a competitor. The regulator restricts scope not because the direct value of transferability becomes negative, but because complete scope induces too large a retreat from common innovation.
