\section{Approved Robustness and Interpretation}

This section records only robustness results approved at the theory freeze. None changes the baseline strategic mechanism.

\subsection{General concave innovation technology}

The directional reallocation results do not depend on the quadratic specification. Let $g$ be increasing and strictly concave on $[0,E]$. The private allocation satisfies
\begin{equation}
 (1-\nu b)g'(x^F)=g'(E-x^F),
\end{equation}
while the planner satisfies
\begin{equation}
 (1+b)g'(x^S)=g'(E-x^S).
\end{equation}
Implicit differentiation gives $dx^F/db<0<dx^S/db$. Hence the private--social directional wedge is a property of the appropriability structure rather than of the quadratic technology.

The exact polynomial threshold in Proposition~\ref{prop:selective} uses the quadratic baseline. The interior-policy result is nevertheless locally robust: in the selective-standardization region the baseline inequalities $F'(0)>0$, $F'(1)<0$, and strict concavity are strict. They therefore persist for sufficiently small $C^2$ perturbations of the innovation technology. We do not claim that every globally concave technology generates an interior policy.

\subsection{Incomplete transferability}

Suppose that the common-layer spillover is $\lambda b g(x_j)$ with $\lambda\in(0,1]$. Defining effective transferability $t=\lambda b$, the private and social first-order conditions become
\begin{equation}
 (1-\nu t)g'(x^F)=g'(E-x^F),
 \qquad
 (1+t)g'(x^S)=g'(E-x^S).
\end{equation}
Thus the directional wedge is unchanged. If the unconstrained optimal effective transferability is $t^*\in(0,1)$, the regulator chooses $b^*=t^*/\lambda<1$ whenever $\lambda>t^*$; if technologically attainable transferability is too weak, $\lambda\leq t^*$, the regulator expands the standard to the feasible maximum. Selective scope therefore requires not only rivalry but a standard capable of making common innovation sufficiently transferable.

\subsection{Endogenous total R\&D}

The fixed-capacity assumption isolates the composition margin. If firms can also choose total R\&D $E=x+z$ subject to a convex capacity cost, the relative first-order condition between common and proprietary effort still takes the form
\begin{equation}
 (1-\nu b)g'(x)=g'(z).
\end{equation}
Hence the private relative-return distortion survives. Total R\&D may itself respond to $b$, so an unrestricted global sign for $dx/db$ no longer follows without additional conditions. When the total-capacity cost is sufficiently steep, however, the endogenous-$E$ model is arbitrarily close to the fixed-$E$ baseline and the strict interior-policy inequalities persist by continuity. We therefore interpret fixed $E$ as a clean way to identify the innovation-composition effect rather than as a knife-edge source of the mechanism.

\subsection{Institutional interpretation}

The variable $b$ is best interpreted as the mandatory scope of a common technological layer or interface. It is not a generic compatibility index and it does not mean that all firm knowledge becomes legally disclosed. The reduced-form spillover in \eqref{eq:quality} represents the greater cross-product usability of improvements made within a standardized layer.

Two application families illustrate the same mechanism without changing the theory. In digital systems, $x$ may represent protocol-, API-, or interface-facing innovation and $z$ proprietary implementation or service innovation. In modular manufacturing, $x$ may represent innovation in standardized external interfaces and $z$ innovation in internal modules or proprietary subsystems. Case evidence from Taiwan's machine-tool industry reports that external interface standards constrain product innovation and that innovative effort tends to concentrate on internal interfaces and modules \citep{ChenLiu2005}. We treat this as suggestive institutional consistency, not as causal validation of the model.

The model yields several empirical predictions. First, an expansion in standard scope should reduce the share of firm R\&D directed toward standardized/common technological classes. Second, this portfolio shift should be stronger where downstream products are closer substitutes, because $\nu$ rises with $\rho$. Third, industries with stronger rivalry should be more likely to exhibit selective rather than exhaustive common technical scope when policymakers internalize innovation incentives. These predictions concern the composition of innovation, not merely total R\&D expenditure.
