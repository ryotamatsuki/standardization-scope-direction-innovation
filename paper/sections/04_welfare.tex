\section{Welfare and Policy Benchmarks}

Because utility is quadratic, consumer surplus can be calculated exactly. At a symmetric Bertrand equilibrium with $A_1=A_2=A$, price and quantity are
\begin{equation}
 p^*=\frac{A(1-\rho)}{2-\rho},
 \qquad
 q^*=\frac{A}{(2-\rho)(1+\rho)}.
 \label{eq:symmetricpq}
\end{equation}
Consumer surplus is
\begin{equation}
 CS(A,\rho)=\frac{A^2}{(2-\rho)^2(1+\rho)},
 \label{eq:CS}
\end{equation}
aggregate producer surplus is
\begin{equation}
 PS(A,\rho)=\frac{2A^2(1-\rho)}{(2-\rho)^2(1+\rho)},
 \label{eq:PS}
\end{equation}
and total surplus is
\begin{equation}
 W(A,\rho)=\frac{A^2(3-2\rho)}{(2-\rho)^2(1+\rho)}.
 \label{eq:W}
\end{equation}
Let
\begin{equation}
 K(\rho)\equiv\frac{3-2\rho}{(2-\rho)^2(1+\rho)}>0.
\end{equation}
Then $W=K(\rho)A^2$, so the regulator's symmetric welfare ranking is identical to its ranking of $A$. The fixed R\&D capacity is policy invariant; any resource cost attached only to that fixed capacity is therefore an additive constant in the policy comparison.

\subsection{Direct diffusion and endogenous portfolio response}

Along the decentralized private R\&D equilibrium, symmetric quality is
\begin{equation}
 A(b)=a+\theta\left[(1+b)g(x^F(b))+g(E-x^F(b))\right].
 \label{eq:Ab}
\end{equation}
In the quadratic interior baseline, differentiating and using \eqref{eq:privategeneral} gives
\begin{equation}
 \frac{dA}{db}
 =\theta\left[
 g(x^F)+b(1+\nu)g'(x^F)\frac{dx^F}{db}
 \right].
 \label{eq:decomposition}
\end{equation}
The first term is the direct diffusion gain from applying common-layer improvements over a broader standardized scope. The second term is zero at $b=0$ and strictly negative for $b>0$ in the quadratic interior baseline because $dx^F/db<0$. Thus positive scope creates a direct transferability gain and an endogenous innovation-composition loss.

Since
\begin{equation}
 \frac{dW}{db}=2K(\rho)A(b)\frac{dA}{db},
\end{equation}
the same decomposition determines the welfare derivative in the symmetric equilibrium. This decomposition does not imply that realized transferred improvement $b g(x^F(b))$ is globally increasing in $b$; the portfolio response can offset the mechanical rise in the transferability coefficient.

\subsection{Coordinated symmetric-R\&D benchmark}

Consider a constrained benchmark in which the regulator chooses both scope $b$ and a common symmetric R\&D composition $x_1=x_2=x$, while differentiated Bertrand pricing remains decentralized. Conditional on $b$, the coordinated R\&D choice solves \eqref{eq:coordinatedproblem}. Let $x^S(b)$ denote its unique maximizer. By the envelope theorem,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{dW^{COORD}}{db}
 =W_A\,\theta g(x^S(b))>0.
\end{equation}
Hence the constrained benchmark selects complete standardization.

\begin{proposition}[Coordinated symmetric-R\&D benchmark]\label{prop:coordinated}
Under the frozen parameter restrictions, if the regulator chooses $b$ and the symmetric R\&D composition while Bertrand pricing remains decentralized, the optimal scope is
\begin{equation}
 b^{COORD}=1.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

This proposition is not an unconstrained first-best result. Even holding symmetric quality $A$ fixed, a planner that controlled quantities would choose
\begin{equation}
 q^{EFF}=\frac{A}{1+\rho}
\end{equation}
and obtain
\begin{equation}
 W^{EFF}(A,\rho)=\frac{A^2}{1+\rho}.
\end{equation}
The decentralized-Bertrand welfare in \eqref{eq:W} is lower by
\begin{equation}
 W^{EFF}-W
 =\frac{A^2(1-\rho)^2}{(2-\rho)^2(1+\rho)}>0.
 \label{eq:bertrandgap}
\end{equation}
Moreover, Proposition~\ref{prop:coordinated} restricts R\&D to the symmetric diagonal. The paper therefore makes no claim about the unconstrained social-planner optimum.

Combining Propositions~\ref{prop:benchmark}, \ref{prop:selective}, and \ref{prop:coordinated} yields the central comparison:
\begin{equation}
 b^{FIX}=b^{COORD}=1,
 \qquad
 0<b^*<1
 \quad\text{when }\bar\nu(y)<\nu<y.
 \label{eq:centralcomparison}
\end{equation}
The contrast is instrument constrained. The regulator would choose complete scope if the symmetric R\&D composition were fixed or directly coordinated, but with scope as the policy instrument and firms subsequently choosing their own portfolios, complete scope can induce an excessive retreat from common-layer innovation. Selective standardization then substitutes imperfectly for a missing instrument that would align private and coordinated returns to common innovation.

The model does not establish that a narrower standard dominates complete standardization combined with an innovation subsidy, licensing rule, disclosure policy, or other appropriation instrument. Those instruments are outside the frozen game. The result is narrower: when standardization scope is the regulator's instrument and R\&D composition is decentralized, the equilibrium portfolio response can make selective scope optimal even though the two constrained comparison exercises select complete scope.
