\section{Introduction}

Technical standards can increase interoperability, diffuse technical knowledge, and enlarge the set of products that can use a common technological improvement. These benefits are central to the economic case for standardization. Yet firms do not passively accept the competitive environment created by a standard. When a broader standard makes an improvement in the common layer more useful to rival products, a firm may respond by shifting innovative effort toward technologies that remain proprietary. The policy question is therefore not only how much interoperability a standard creates directly, but also how the scope of the standard changes the direction of innovation.

This paper studies that feedback in a deliberately stripped-down industrial-organization model. A regulator first chooses the scope of a common technical standard. Two firms then allocate a fixed R\&D capacity between a common layer and a proprietary layer, after which they compete in differentiated Bertrand prices. Common-layer R\&D raises the innovating firm's product quality, but a fraction determined by the scope of the standard also raises the quality of the rival's standard-compliant product. Proprietary R\&D benefits only the innovating firm. The standard therefore changes the appropriability of the two uses of a scarce innovation budget without changing total R\&D capacity by assumption.

The model produces a sharp private--social reallocation wedge. A broader standard lowers the private marginal return to common-layer R\&D because the induced spillover strengthens the rival in the product market. Firms consequently shift R\&D from the common layer toward proprietary innovation. A social planner moves in the opposite direction: the same spillover that hurts the innovating firm expands the social value of common-layer innovation. Thus standardization reduces private common-layer R\&D precisely when it raises the socially desirable amount of such R\&D.

The main result concerns the regulator's scope choice. If the R\&D composition is held fixed and includes a positive amount of common-layer innovation, welfare is increasing in the scope of the standard, so complete standardization is optimal. Complete standardization is also first-best when the regulator can control both the standard and the R\&D allocation. With decentralized R\&D allocation, however, a broader standard induces firms to retreat from common innovation. When product-market rivalry is sufficiently strong, this endogenous portfolio response outweighs the direct diffusion gain near complete standardization. The regulator then chooses a unique interior standardization scope. Selective standardization is therefore a second-best response to an innovation-composition distortion, not a statement that interoperability is intrinsically undesirable.

This result is distinct from several established insights. Endogenous product design can change the welfare consequences of compatibility \citep{Boom2001}, and government standardization policy can interact with endogenous product characteristics \citep{Ruiz2004}. Compatibility and innovation incentives have also been studied in models with endogenous R\&D and network effects \citep{BondSmith2019,MaruyamaZennyo2017,HeywoodWangYe2022}, while standard-setting institutions can generate insufficient or excessive innovation \citep{Llanes2024}. More generally, standardization can itself trade off against innovation in dynamic environments \citep{AcemogluGanciaZilibotti2012}. In a different literature, firms allocate scarce innovative resources across internal and external or joint and proprietary activities \citep{KretschmerReitzig2013,daSilva2024}. Our contribution is not any one of these broad mechanisms. It is the full-game result that regulator-selected standardization scope changes the private composition of a fixed R\&D budget in the opposite direction from the social allocation, and that this endogenous composition response alone can change the regulator's optimum from complete to selective standardization.

The mechanism is intended to apply to technological settings with a meaningful common interface or standardized layer and a distinct proprietary layer. Examples include digital interfaces and modular industrial products. Evidence from modular product development is consistent with innovative effort moving toward internal interfaces and modules when external interfaces are standardized \citep{ChenLiu2005}. We treat such evidence as motivation rather than as a direct test of the model.

The analysis proceeds in four steps. Section~2 defines the game and establishes conditions under which every upstream history has a unique active-product Bertrand continuation. Section~3 characterizes private R\&D allocation and derives the threshold under which endogenous R\&D turns complete standardization into a unique interior policy. Section~4 derives exact consumer surplus and total welfare and separates the first-best and second-best policy problems. Section~5 records the approved robustness results, and Section~6 positions the mechanism relative to the closest literatures.
